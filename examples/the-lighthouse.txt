In 1968, on a narrow spit of rock off the coast of Maine, a lighthouse keeper named Harold Pruett made a decision that would puzzle historians for fifty years. He stopped writing in his logbook.

For eleven years, Harold had recorded everything. Wind speeds. Passing ships. The exact minute the lamp was lit each evening. Eleven years of meticulous, unbroken entries. And then, on the night of March 14th, the log simply ends. Mid-sentence.

The Coast Guard found him three weeks later, alive and well, tending the light as if nothing had happened. When they asked about the logbook, Harold said only this: I ran out of things worth saying.

But here's where the story turns. Decades later, after Harold's death, his granddaughter Ruth found a second logbook, hidden beneath a floorboard in the lamp room. It began on March 15th, 1968. The night after the official record stopped.

This one contained no wind speeds. No ships. Instead, page after page described the birds. Storm petrels, mostly, that circled the light on foggy nights. Harold had given them names. He recorded their arrivals like old friends checking into a hotel. He wrote about one bird, which he called Admiral, for nine consecutive years.

Ruth published the hidden logbook last spring. It has since sold two hundred thousand copies. Ornithologists say Harold's observations filled a gap in migration science that no researcher had even known was there.

Harold Pruett never ran out of things worth saying. He had simply decided, one night in March, to say them only to himself.
